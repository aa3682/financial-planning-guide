# About this guide

This page explains what the guide is for, how it is put together, and where its limits are. It also tells you how to report a mistake and what you may do with the material.

## What this is

This is a free, public reference on personal financial planning. It is written for the general public first and for practicing financial advisors second, so every page starts from plain English and marks its practitioner depth where that depth appears.

The site is organized by the planning process at the top level: seven steps, in the order a plan actually follows. Beneath that sit eight knowledge areas, the bodies of knowledge the steps draw on, and a set of tools that make the work concrete. You can read the steps in order or go straight to the area that answers your question.

It exists because most planning material is either written for professionals or written to sell something, and a reader who wants neither has few places to go.

## What it is not

This guide is educational. It describes how personal financial planning works in general, not what you should do. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy, sell, or hold any product, or to take any specific action. Nothing here accounts for your situation: your income, your family, your state, your tax return, or what you already have in place. A decision about your own money belongs with someone who knows the facts of it, whether that is you after your own homework or a professional you have hired to look.

## How figures are handled

The body pages on this site never print a limit, bracket, age, threshold, rate, or deadline set by law or by a government agency. Those numbers change, usually once a year, and a figure printed in the middle of a paragraph goes stale without anyone noticing. Instead, a page names the concept in plain words, such as "up to the yearly limit," and links to the one page that holds the number.

That page is [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures). Every figure there sits in its own row with the year it applies to and a link to the primary source it was taken from. The page is checked against its sources once a year, so a stale number has one place to be found and one place to be fixed.

## How sources are chosen

When a page needs support for a claim, it cites a public primary source rather than a secondary summary. In practice that means statutes and regulations, IRS publications, SEC and FINRA rules, explainer pages from federal agencies such as the FDIC, the CFPB, and the Social Security Administration, and academic papers cited by their DOI so you can find the exact version. Commentary, marketing material, and other people's guides are not used as sources, however good they are. If a claim cannot be traced to a source of that kind, it is either rewritten as an opinion or left out.

## Who writes it

The guide is written and maintained by one practitioner in personal financial planning. It carries no name or employer on purpose: the material should stand or fall on its sources and its clarity, not on who wrote it. All of the writing is original, and no page reproduces text from any textbook, study guide, or publication.

## Corrections

If you find an error, a broken link, or a figure that is out of date, report it on the repository's [Issues page](https://github.com/aa3682/financial-planning-guide/issues). Say which page and which line, and if you can, link the source that shows the correct fact. Reports are read, but a reply is not guaranteed.
