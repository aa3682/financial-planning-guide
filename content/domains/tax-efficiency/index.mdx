# Tax efficiency

Tax efficiency is the knowledge area about keeping more of what you earn and grow, within the rules. The levers are ordinary: which account holds a dollar, when a gain is taken, which year a deduction lands. None changes what you earn, only what you keep.

## Key ideas

### How the tiers work

Federal income tax is charged in tiers. Only the income inside a tier is taxed at that tier's rate, so crossing into a higher tier never lowers your take-home pay. The rate on your highest tier is your top rate, the [marginal rate](/glossary#marginal-rate). Your overall rate, the [effective rate](/glossary#effective-rate), is total tax divided by total income, and is always lower. The next dollar earned or deducted is taxed at the top rate, so that is the rate that matters. The real tiers change yearly (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency)).

Take Priya, with $80,000 of taxable income under illustrative tiers: 10 percent on the first $20,000, 20 percent on the next $40,000, and 30 percent above that. Her tax is $2,000 plus $8,000 plus $6,000, or $16,000: an overall rate of 20 percent. Her top rate is 30, so a $1,000 raise costs her $300.

### Deductions and credits

A [deduction](/glossary#deduction) removes income before the tiers apply, so it is worth your top rate: a $1,000 deduction saves Priya $300. A [credit](/glossary#credit) comes off the tax itself, so a $1,000 credit saves $1,000 at any rate. Every filer gets a flat [standard deduction](/glossary#standard-deduction); listing individual deductions instead, called itemizing, pays only when they add up to more. The standard amount is on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Paying as the year goes

Tax is owed as income arrives, not at filing. For wages, your employer holds back a share of each paycheck, called [withholding](/glossary#withholding). Other income, such as self-employment or rent, is paid through [estimated payments](/glossary#estimated-payments) four times a year. Pay too little through the year and a penalty applies at filing; the safe level, due dates, and filing deadline are on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Three ways an account can be taxed

A [taxable account](/glossary#taxable-account) is taxed as you go: interest and dividends as they arrive, gains when you sell, with no limits and no withdrawal rules. A [tax-deferred account](/glossary#tax-deferred-account) takes money before tax, grows untaxed, and taxes every withdrawal as ordinary income; it suits a top rate today higher than you expect later. The third kind takes money already taxed and never taxes the growth; it suits the reverse.

The last two are the [tax-advantaged accounts](/glossary#tax-advantaged-account) from [Growing your money](/domains/growing-your-money). Their price is a yearly contribution limit, an extra tax on withdrawals before a set age, and, for the deferred kind, required withdrawals from a set age (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway)).

### Which account to fill first

The [funding order](/glossary#funding-order) for savings follows the arithmetic. An [employer match](/glossary#employer-match) is an immediate return nothing else offers, so it comes first. High-cost debt comes next: paying off a balance at 20 percent is a guaranteed 20 percent return. Then tax-advantaged accounts up to their limits, deferred or never-taxed by the rate comparison above, and a taxable account for the rest. How much to save is [Retirement runway](/domains/retirement-runway)'s question.

### What goes where

[Asset location](/glossary#asset-location) is which holdings sit in which kind of account. Holdings that throw off taxable income every year, such as bonds and funds that trade often, cost least inside a tax-advantaged account. Holdings that mostly grow without paying out, such as broad stock index funds, sit cheaply in a taxable account, where tax waits until you sell, at the lower long-term rate. Location comes after the mix is chosen, never instead of it.

### Gains, losses, and holding period

When you sell for more than you paid, the difference is a [capital gain](/glossary#capital-gain); what you paid is your [cost basis](/glossary#cost-basis). A gain is [realized](/glossary#realized), and taxed, only when you sell. Gains on holdings kept longer than a set period get lower long-term rates; shorter holds are taxed as ordinary income at your top rate.

Losses offset gains, then a limited amount of ordinary income each year; the rest carries forward. Selling a loser to bank the loss is [tax-loss harvesting](/glossary#tax-loss-harvesting); buying the same holding back within a short window undoes it. The period, rates, offset, window, and an extra tax on investment income above an income threshold are on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Timing, other layers, and records

Tiers apply per year, so moving income or a deduction across a year boundary changes the bill. A bonus or sale delayed into a lower-income year, such as a job gap or early retirement, is taxed at a lower top rate, and two years of deductions bunched into one can clear the standard deduction.

State and local governments add a separate layer: some states charge no income tax, some a flat rate, some their own tiers, with their own rules on deductions. The federal deduction for state and local taxes paid is capped (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency)).

Keep the records: returns, the yearly forms from employers and brokers, and receipts behind every deduction, contribution, and basis figure. The government has a set number of years to question a return, and basis records must outlast the holding by that period.

## Common misconceptions

- **A raise into a higher tier can leave you worse off.** Only the dollars above the line are taxed more.
- **A big refund means the year went well.** It means you overpaid every month.
- **Retirement accounts are tax-free.** Most are tax-deferred; the tax waits.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
Here you are mapping the household's tax position, not preparing its return. Document this year's top rate and the expected retirement rate, each account's tax treatment and basis, withholding against projected liability, loss carryforwards, and state of residence. Coordinate with the tax preparer before shifting income between years.
