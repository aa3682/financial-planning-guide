# Growing your money

Growing your money is the knowledge area about putting savings to work: accepting some risk of loss in exchange for growth that cash alone cannot deliver. It covers what you can own, why many holdings beat a few, what it costs, and how to match the mix to a goal. Much of the skill is in deciding what to leave alone.

## Key ideas

### Saving and investing are different jobs

[Saving](/glossary#saving) sets money aside where it keeps its value and stays reachable. [Investing](/glossary#investing) puts money at risk of loss in the hope of ending up with more. You take the risk because safe money barely keeps pace with [inflation](/glossary#inflation), and a distant goal needs growth. The rule connecting the two is the trade between [risk](/glossary#risk) and [expected return](/glossary#expected-return): investments that can fall further are priced to pay more, on average, to those who hold them through the falls. Expected means an average over many years, not a promise for any one of them. Money needed within a few years belongs in savings; there is no time to recover from a bad stretch.

### What people own

| Holding | What it is | What it does in a mix |
|---|---|---|
| Cash-like holdings | Bank deposits, money-market funds, short-term government bills | Keeps value, stays reachable, earns little |
| Bonds | Loans to a government or company that pay interest and return the principal on a set date | Steadier income; cushions stock falls |
| Stocks | Ownership shares in companies | Highest long-run growth; largest swings |
| Real estate | Property held directly or through a fund | Income plus growth; tied to local markets |
| Pooled funds | One fund that holds many of the above | Spreads money widely with a single purchase |

A [bond](/glossary#bond) is a loan you make; a [stock](/glossary#stock) is a slice of a business you own. Bonds are steadier because the payments are fixed in advance. Stocks grow more, and swing more, because their owners are paid last. A [pooled fund](/glossary#pooled-fund), a mutual fund or an exchange-traded fund, is how most people hold any of these: one purchase buys hundreds of holdings.

### Spreading the money

[Diversification](/glossary#diversification) means holding many investments rather than a few. The risk of any single company, a lawsuit, a failed product, a fraud, earns you nothing extra, and owning its competitors too removes it at no cost in expected return. What remains is the risk of the whole market falling, which cannot be spread away and is the risk you are paid to bear. Spread across companies, industries, countries, and kinds of holding.

An [index fund](/glossary#index-fund) takes this to its conclusion: it holds every company on a published list, in proportion. Picking individual stocks, or paying a manager to pick them, is a bet on beating the average. Before costs, all investors together earn exactly the average; after costs, the average picker earns less. Some do beat it; telling skill from luck in advance is very hard.

### What investing costs

Three costs recur. Fund expenses are a yearly percentage taken from the fund's assets, quoted as an [expense ratio](/glossary#expense-ratio). Trading costs are paid each time you buy or sell. Advice fees pay a person or firm, usually as a percentage of the money they oversee. Each looks small, but a cost paid every year comes out of a balance that would otherwise have kept growing, so over decades it compounds against you. One point a year over thirty years removes roughly a sixth of the final balance.

### Matching the mix to you

[Asset allocation](/glossary#asset-allocation) is the split among the kinds of holding above. Your [time horizon](/glossary#time-horizon) is how long until the money is spent: the longer it is, the more can sit in stocks, since there is time to recover from a fall. Your [risk tolerance](/glossary#risk-tolerance) is how large a drop you can live through without selling, judged by what you did in the last one. A mix you would abandon in a bad year is the wrong mix, whatever its long-run average.

Take Marcus, who saves $300 a month for thirty years. His contributions total $108,000. At 6 percent a year, an illustration and not a forecast, the balance would reach about $300,000. If yearly costs took one point of that, leaving 5 percent, the same contributions reach about $250,000. The gap between them is fees; the gap between either and $108,000 is [compounding](/glossary#compounding).

A strong year for stocks leaves Marcus with more of them than he chose. [Rebalancing](/glossary#rebalancing) sells what has grown and buys what has lagged, once a year or when the split drifts past a set band.

### What you control

You cannot control when markets rise and fall, or next year's return, and nobody can reliably time either. You can control [how much you save](/domains/retirement-runway), what you pay, how widely you spread, and whether you stay put in a bad year. Those four decide most of the outcome, and each is a habit rather than a prediction.

### Where the money lives

The account an investment sits in decides how much of each year's return stays invested to compound. In a taxable account, part of the interest, dividends, and gains goes to tax each year before it can grow. In a [tax-advantaged account](/glossary#tax-advantaged-account), the whole return keeps compounding, in exchange for a yearly cap on what you can add and rules on when you can take it out (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway)). Over decades that gap compounds too, so for a distant goal the account matters nearly as much as the mix inside it. How each kind of account is taxed, and which to fill first, is in [Tax efficiency](/domains/tax-efficiency#three-ways-an-account-can-be-taxed).

## Common misconceptions

- **A good year proves a good choice.** Short-term results are mostly noise. Judge a decision by its process and cost.
- **You have to watch the market to invest well.** Watching produces trading, and trading produces costs.
- **Cash is the safe choice for every goal.** For money needed soon, yes. Held for decades, cash reliably loses purchasing power.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
Here you are setting and documenting the client's investment policy, not picking winners. Record the target allocation with the horizon and risk tolerance behind it, how tolerance was assessed and what the client did in the last downturn, the rebalancing rule, and the portfolio's all-in cost as a percentage. Label every assumed return as an assumption. A written policy lets a later review separate a plan that failed from a market that did what markets do.
</Callout>
