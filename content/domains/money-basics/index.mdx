# Money basics

Money basics is the knowledge area behind everyday household finance: what comes in and goes out, what you own and owe, the cash you keep in reserve and where you keep it, how borrowing works, and how time changes the value of a dollar. All of it is load-bearing, because every other knowledge area assumes these facts and habits are already in place.

## Key ideas

### What comes in and what goes out

[Cash flow](/glossary) is the movement of money through your household over a period, usually a month: income arriving, spending leaving, and what is left. The last number matters most. If more leaves than arrives, savings or borrowing covers the gap, and both run out. Track it from statements, not memory. A steady positive gap is what funds every goal in the plan.

### What you own and what you owe

A balance sheet is a snapshot rather than a flow. On one side sit your [assets](/glossary), everything you own that has value. On the other sit your [liabilities](/glossary), everything you owe. Assets minus liabilities is your [net worth](/glossary). It can be negative early on and still be healthy. What matters is the direction it moves when you measure it again.

### A cash cushion, and how large

A [cash reserve](/glossary), often called an emergency fund, is money set aside to absorb a shock without borrowing or selling investments at a bad moment: a car repair, a medical bill, a gap between jobs. Its right size is not a fixed number. It depends on how steady your income is, how quickly you could replace it, and what insurance already covers. Size it in months of essential spending, and keep it where you can reach it in days without penalty.

### Where cash lives

Cash belongs in accounts that are safe, reachable, and insured, whether at a bank or a member-owned credit union. Deposits at a federally insured bank are covered by [deposit insurance](/glossary), a government guarantee that you get your money back up to a limit if the institution fails. Credit unions have an equivalent program through a separate federal agency. The limit applies per depositor, per institution, per category of ownership, so more cash than the limit can stay covered by spreading it; see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#money-basics). Investments held at the same institution are not covered.

### Borrowing and credit

Borrowing brings future income into the present and charges you for it. The charge is the [interest rate](/glossary), and its most honest form is the [APR](/glossary), the annual percentage rate, which folds fees into the rate so two loans can be compared. Lenders decide whether to lend, and at what rate, from your [credit report](/glossary), a record of your borrowing and repayment kept by private reporting companies, summarized as a [credit score](/glossary). Paying on time and using only a modest share of your available credit are what move the score. You can see your own report free at a set frequency (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#money-basics)).

Debt divides into cheap and costly. A mortgage or a subsidized student loan is cheap: a low rate, a long term, and usually an asset behind it. Credit-card balances and payday loans are costly: high rates that compound against you every month. When paying down several debts, the arithmetic favors the highest rate first while paying the minimum on the rest. Clearing the smallest balance first is a common alternative; either order beats no order.

### How money grows, and shrinks, over time

[Compounding](/glossary) is growth on growth: interest earned this year earns its own interest next year. Over a decade or two the growth itself becomes most of the balance, which is why starting early matters more than starting big. The same engine runs in reverse on unpaid debt.

[Inflation](/glossary) is the slow rise in prices that makes a fixed sum buy less each year. Cash that earns nothing loses purchasing power silently, so a reserve held for years should earn close to the measured rate, which is on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#money-basics). Together they set the cost of waiting: a goal deferred needs more money later, and idle money earns nothing toward it.

## Common misconceptions

- **A budget is the same as tracking spending.** A budget is a plan; tracking is a record. Most people need the record first.
- **A cash cushion is wasted money.** It earns little, but it lets the rest of the plan take sensible risk. Without it, every shock goes on a credit card.
- **Carrying a balance builds credit.** Paying in full and on time builds credit. Interest paid buys nothing.
- **Small amounts are not worth saving.** Compounding rewards time more than size; small and early often beats large and late.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
In this area you are establishing the household's operating facts and habits, not its strategy. Document cash flow from at least three months of statements, the reserve's target size and why, where it sits and whether it is fully insured, and every debt with its rate, term, and minimum payment. Record the client's stated payoff order and whether it matches the rate-first arithmetic; a deliberate departure is fine, an unnoticed one is not. These facts are the baseline later assumptions are measured against.
</Callout>
