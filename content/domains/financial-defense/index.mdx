# Financial defense

Financial defense is the knowledge area about losses you could not absorb: a house fire, a long illness, a lawsuit, a death that takes an income with it. Insurance moves such a risk onto a pool of people who share it, for a price. The skill is knowing which risks to move, which to keep, and how to read the contract.

## Key ideas

### How insurance works

An insurer collects a [premium](/glossary#premium), the price of the policy, from many people facing the same risk and pays the few who suffer the loss. This is [risk pooling](/glossary#risk-pooling): your small certain payment replaces a large uncertain one. Three terms decide how much the pool pays: the [deductible](/glossary#deductible), the first slice of any loss you pay yourself; the [policy limit](/glossary#policy-limit), the most the insurer will pay; and the [exclusions](/glossary#exclusions), the causes it never covers. A higher deductible lowers the premium, because you keep more of the risk.

Most people pay in more than they get out; what you buy is the certainty that one bad year cannot end the plan.

### What to insure and what to carry yourself

The test is size, not likelihood. Insure the losses that would wreck your finances even if they are rare. Carry the small ones yourself even if they are common; premiums on predictable losses cost more than the losses. The [cash reserve](/glossary#cash-reserve) described under [Money basics](/domains/money-basics) pays the deductibles, the waiting periods, and the losses too small to insure; a larger reserve lets you take higher deductibles and pocket the savings.

Take Dana, who keeps a $12,000 reserve. Her auto insurer offers a $500 deductible for $1,400 a year or a $1,000 deductible for $1,250. The higher deductible saves $150 every year and costs an extra $500 only in a year with a claim, which her reserve absorbs easily, so she takes it. The reserve could not absorb a $300,000 house fire or three years without a paycheck, so those risks stay in the pool.

### The main kinds of protection

| Kind | What it protects against |
|---|---|
| Health | Medical bills, the largest risk here |
| Disability income | Losing your paycheck to illness or injury |
| Life | Dependents losing your income or care |
| Home or renters | Damage to the building and belongings |
| Auto | Harm you cause on the road, and damage to your car |
| Personal liability | Claims beyond home and auto limits |
| Long-term care | Paying for help with daily living |

Health and disability policies pay you. A life policy pays a [beneficiary](/glossary#beneficiary). [Liability](/glossary#liability) coverage pays someone else on your behalf; state law sets a minimum for auto, usually far below what a serious crash costs (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#financial-defense)). Long-term care is the risk most often left uncovered: Medicare and Medicaid pay only for short recoveries, or only once your assets fall below a floor (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#financial-defense)).

### Reading a policy

The declarations page at the front of a policy summarizes the contract. Read the rest for four things: the deductible, the limit, the exclusions, and what triggers a payment. For disability, the trigger matters most: whether "disabled" means unable to do your own job or any job, and the wait before payments start, the [elimination period](/glossary#elimination-period). Health plans cap what you pay in a year, and regulation sets the cap (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#financial-defense)). For home and renters, check whether belongings are paid at replacement cost or used value. Exclusions are where surprises live; floods and earthquakes are commonly left out.

### Employer coverage versus your own

Coverage through work is often cheap or free, and for health it is usually the best deal available. Its weakness is that it ends when the job does, and the employer sets the amounts, and group disability usually replaces only part of your pay, taxed if the employer paid the premium. Your own policy follows you between jobs and is sized to you, at a higher price. Employer coverage makes a good base, with your own policy on top where the gap is largest. Federal law lets you keep job-based health coverage for a limited period after leaving, at your own cost, and Social Security pays a disability benefit for severe lasting conditions after a waiting period; both are on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#financial-defense).

### Reviewing as life changes

Protection is sized to a life, and the life moves. A marriage, a child, a house, a business, a divorce, or a retirement changes who depends on you and what you could lose. Review every policy at each such event and once a year regardless: amounts against income and debts, beneficiaries against current wishes, coverage against anything new.

## Common misconceptions

- **Insurance is a bad deal because you usually lose money.** You are meant to. It buys certainty, not a return.
- **Young and healthy means no coverage needed.** At working ages a long disability is likelier than an early death, and policies are cheapest while you are healthy.
- **The employer plan covers me.** Only while you are employed, and only at amounts the employer chose.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
Here you are matching coverage to exposures, not placing policies. Document the losses that would exceed the reserve, each policy's limits, deductibles, elimination periods, and exclusions, and the gap between the two. Record each disability definition, each life beneficiary against the estate documents, and whether group coverage is portable. Note the client's chosen retention level and why, so a later review can tell a deliberate gap from an overlooked one.
</Callout>
