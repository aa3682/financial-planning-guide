# Passing on wealth

Passing on wealth is the knowledge area about what happens to what you own when you die, and who acts for you if you cannot. Most of it is governed by the law of the state you live in, so details vary; the pieces and how they fit together do not.

## Key ideas

### Who decides

Everything you own at death is your [estate](/glossary#estate). A [will](/glossary#will) is your written instruction for who gets it, who carries that out, and, for parents, who raises minor children: a court appoints the [guardian](/glossary#guardian) and gives weight to the parent's choice. Without a will, the state's default rules decide, in a fixed order by relationship: spouse, children, parents, siblings, outward. The defaults suit a first marriage with shared children; a partner you never married, a stepchild you raised, and a friend get nothing.

### What the will does not control

A will reaches only what has no other route. Retirement accounts and life insurance pay whoever is named as [beneficiary](/glossary#beneficiary) on the account's own form. A joint account with a [right of survivorship](/glossary#right-of-survivorship) passes to the other owner. An account marked to [transfer on death](/glossary#transfer-on-death) goes to the person marked. Each overrides the will, and each was set by a form filled in years ago. That is the most common place plans go wrong: the will is updated after a divorce or a birth, the forms are not, and the forms win. [Retitling](/glossary#retitling), changing whose name is on an account or deed, is how ownership itself moves.

Take Omar, whose will, signed last year, leaves everything to his two children. His largest asset is a retirement account whose beneficiary form still names a former spouse. The account goes to the former spouse. The children get what the will controls, which is everything else. The will was correct, and it did not matter.

### Settling the estate

[Probate](/glossary#probate) is the court process that proves the will, appoints the [executor](/glossary#executor) who carries it out, pays debts and taxes, and hands out what remains. It is public, takes months to a year or more, and costs court and legal fees that vary by state and by how contested it gets. Assets with a named beneficiary, a surviving joint owner, or a transfer-on-death marking skip it; so does anything held in a trust.

### Trusts

A [trust](/glossary#trust) is a container. Someone puts property in, a [trustee](/glossary#trustee) manages it, and a written document says what the trustee may do and for whom. A trust you can change or cancel keeps everything in your control; it mainly avoids probate and keeps instructions private and in force through incapacity, and for tax purposes the property is still yours. A trust you cannot change gives the property up for good, which is why it can move property out of your estate. Either kind covers only property actually retitled into it.

### If you cannot act

A durable [power of attorney](/glossary#power-of-attorney) names someone to handle your money: pay bills, file taxes, deal with accounts. "Durable" means it keeps working after you lose the ability to decide for yourself. A [health-care directive](/glossary#health-care-directive) names someone to make medical decisions and records the treatment you want or refuse. Without them, a family member must ask a court for that authority. Both end at death; the will and the forms take over from there.

### Giving, and the taxes involved

You can give any person a yearly amount without reporting it, and gifts above that are reported and count against a lifetime allowance rather than taxed at once (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#passing-on-wealth)). People give during life to see the money used, to help when it is needed, or to shrink an estate that would otherwise be taxed.

The federal estate tax applies only above a threshold high enough that most estates owe nothing, and transfers between spouses are unlimited (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#passing-on-wealth)). Some states charge their own tax on estates, or on the people who inherit, at far lower thresholds, listed on the same page.

Gifts to charity at death, by will or by naming the charity as beneficiary, leave the estate tax-free. Investments inherited at death get a new [cost basis](/glossary#cost-basis) equal to their value that day, so the gain built up over the owner's life is never taxed; the same shares given during life keep the old basis. That difference is why giving now versus leaving later often turns on which asset it is. [Tax efficiency](/domains/tax-efficiency) covers basis and gains.

### Insurance, and keeping it all current

Life insurance creates money exactly when an estate needs it: to replace an income, pay taxes or debts, or leave something to a person who gets nothing else. [Financial defense](/domains/financial-defense) covers the policy itself.

Documents work only if they are current and findable: who holds the originals, where the account list is, and who knows. A marriage, divorce, birth, death, move to another state, or large change in wealth is a reason to reread the will, the forms, and the named people; [Stay on course](/process/stay-on-course) builds that into the review.

## Common misconceptions

- **A will covers everything.** It covers only what has no beneficiary form, joint owner, or trust.
- **Estate tax is a problem for most families.** The federal threshold is far above most estates; state taxes are the likelier surprise.
- **Documents are for old people.** Incapacity and guardianship questions fall hardest on the young with children.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
Here you are coordinating, not drafting; the documents come from an attorney licensed in the client's state. Record every account's registration and beneficiary designation as shown on the custodian's records, and reconcile each against the will and any trust. Note which documents exist, their dates, who holds each role, where the originals are, and which assets would pass through probate today. Flag any trust with property never retitled into it. Confirm with the attorney and tax preparer before any gift or retitling.
</Callout>
