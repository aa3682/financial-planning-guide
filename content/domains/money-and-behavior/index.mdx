# Money and behavior

Money and behavior is the knowledge area about the gap between what people know and what they do. Most money mistakes are made by people who knew the right answer, for reasons that are predictable and shared. This page covers those reasons, where they show up, and what tends to close the gap.

## Key ideas

### Knowing and doing are different problems

Understanding compounding does not make anyone save. Knowing that markets recover does not make a fall feel survivable while it is happening. The first is a knowledge problem, which reading solves. The second is a behavior problem, which reading does not touch. A plan that solves only the first exists on paper.

### The patterns that show up most

A small set of tendencies accounts for most of the gap.

| What happens | Technical name | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| A loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases | [Loss aversion](/glossary#loss-aversion) | Selling after a fall to stop the hurt; holding a loser to avoid making the loss real |
| Now pulls harder than later | [Present bias](/glossary#present-bias) | Spending rising to meet income; paperwork with no deadline never getting done |
| A number you happened to see becomes the reference point | [Anchoring](/glossary#anchoring) | Judging a price against what it was, not what it is worth |
| Money is treated differently depending on which mental bucket it sits in | [Mental accounting](/glossary#mental-accounting) | A refund spent freely while a credit card balance grows |
| What others are doing feels like evidence | [Herding](/glossary#herding) | Chasing last year's winner; buying whatever everyone is talking about |
| Your own judgment gets more credit than the record supports | [Overconfidence](/glossary#overconfidence) | Frequent trading; skipping insurance because it will not happen to you |
| Recent events count for more than older ones | [Recency bias](/glossary#recency-bias) | A good year makes stocks feel safe; a bad one makes them feel doomed |

These are features of how people in general weigh choices, found across countries, incomes, and levels of education. Professionals show them too, which is why the fix is rarely to know more.

### Why structure beats willpower

Willpower is a resource that runs down through the day and runs out in a crisis, which is exactly when the money decision arrives. Structure does not run out: decisions made once, in calm, then run without anyone deciding again.

A [default](/glossary#default) is what happens if you do nothing. Workplace plans that enroll people automatically, and let them opt out, end up with far more savers than plans that ask people to opt in, even though the choice is identical. An [automatic transfer](/glossary#automatic-transfer) moves a set amount on a set day, so saving happens before spending gets a vote. A rule decided in advance, such as a fixed split between stocks and bonds or a written list of what would justify a change, means that a bad month is compared against a rule instead of a mood. Friction works both ways: less on the choices that should be easy, more on the ones that should be hard, such as a savings account with no card attached. [Put it in motion](/process/put-it-in-motion) is the step where this structure gets built.

Take Priya, who holds $100,000 in a broad stock fund when the market falls by a quarter, to $75,000. The fall hurts more than the earlier gains felt good, and she sells to make it stop. Over the next eighteen months the market rises 40 percent from that low, an illustrative figure and not a forecast. Had she stayed, she would hold about $105,000. Instead she returns when the recovery feels safe, after the market has already climbed 30 percent, and her $75,000 catches only the last leg, ending near $81,000. The gap of roughly $24,000 is the cost of one pattern, paid once.

### Where habits come from

Money habits are learned early and mostly without instruction. People who grew up in a household where money was scarce, or never discussed, or a source of conflict, tend to carry the reflexes that made sense there into circumstances where they no longer fit. Someone raised amid shortage may keep hoarding after the shortage has ended. Someone who saw money used to keep score may spend to signal. This is a fact about people, not a verdict on anyone. A habit with a visible origin is easier to treat as a habit than as a character.

### Money inside relationships

Two people rarely share the same instincts. One saves by reflex and the other spends by reflex, and each reads the other's habit as a flaw. Most money fights are not about the amount. They are about what the money stands for: safety, freedom, fairness, respect. Couples who manage it well tend to have agreed on the rules before the disagreement arrived: what each can spend without asking, what counts as a shared goal, and what triggers a conversation. The rules do the arguing so the people do not have to. [Set goals](/process/set-goals) is where those shared goals get written down.

### Timing, waiting periods, and a second pair of eyes

Decisions made under stress lean on the patterns above. A large purchase, a career change, a move out of the market, an inheritance: each goes better decided when nothing is on fire. For choices that cannot be undone, a waiting period set in advance, a week or a month, costs almost nothing and catches most of the mistakes that come from a bad day.

An outside person adds something no amount of self-awareness supplies: a view of your reasoning from outside it. A partner, a friend who will ask the obvious question, or a planner all serve as a check, and a planner's largest contribution is often not the plan but the conversation that stops a sale in a bad week. [Working with a planner](/domains/working-with-a-planner) covers that role. The [review](/glossary#review) in [Stay on course](/process/stay-on-course) does the same job on a schedule, comparing what happened with what was planned so that drift is caught before it compounds.

## Common misconceptions

- **Smart people are immune.** The patterns show up in experts and professionals at roughly the same rates.
- **The fix is more discipline.** Discipline is what fails under stress. Structure is what holds.
- **Feeling calm means the decision is sound.** The patterns feel like clear thinking from the inside.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
Here you are designing for the client's behavior, not just their balance sheet. Record what the client did in the last downturn, which decisions they say they regret, and the household rules a couple has agreed on. Write the investment policy so that a future conversation can point at a sentence the client wrote in calm. Build the automatic pieces first, before any that depend on follow-through. Note when a decision arrived under stress and the waiting period agreed for it, and at each review compare behavior with plan before comparing balances.
</Callout>
