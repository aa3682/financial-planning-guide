# Retirement runway

Retirement runway is the knowledge area about the day your paycheck stops and what replaces it: a stretch of years of unknown length, paid for by [guaranteed income](/glossary#guaranteed-income), payments that arrive for life, plus withdrawals from what you saved. This page owns the question the other areas hand off: how much you need, and what that means for what you save today.

## Key ideas

### Three sources of income

Most people draw on three. The first is Social Security, the federal benefit you earn by paying into it from your wages: a monthly amount for life that rises with inflation and continues to a surviving spouse. The second is an employer [pension](/glossary#pension), a lifetime monthly payment set by pay and years of service. The third is your own savings, in workplace, individual retirement, and ordinary accounts. Only the first two are guaranteed.

### How the federal benefit works

You qualify by working and paying in for enough years (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway)). The monthly amount is set by your lifetime earnings, then adjusted by the age you start. Start at the earliest allowed age and the payment is permanently reduced; wait past your full benefit age and it is permanently increased, up to a latest age after which waiting adds nothing (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway)). Waiting means fewer checks, each larger, that keep paying if you outlive the average. Health, other income, and a surviving spouse all bear on the choice, which lasts for life.

### How workplace plans work

A workplace plan takes a share of each paycheck before you see it, a deferral, into an account in your name. Many employers add an [employer match](/glossary#employer-match), a contribution tied to yours up to a percentage of pay. Your deferrals are always yours; the match often becomes yours only after a set number of years, called [vesting](/glossary#vesting). Law caps vesting periods and yearly deferrals (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway)). When you change jobs, the account can stay, move into the new plan, or move into an individual account through a [rollover](/glossary#rollover), which keeps its tax status. Cashing out instead triggers tax and, before a set age, an extra tax.

### How much is enough

Start from expected spending in retirement, not income today. Subtract what guaranteed income will cover. What remains is the yearly gap savings must fill, for as many years as you might live. The gap, multiplied up, is the target; the target, spread over the years until you stop working, is your [savings rate](/glossary#savings-rate) today.

Take Lena, 45, who expects to spend $60,000 a year in retirement, in today's dollars. Her benefit estimate is $24,000 and she has no pension, so the gap is $36,000. At a withdrawal rate of 4 percent, an illustration and not a rule, savings of $900,000 would cover it. She has $250,000. Growing at 5 percent a year after inflation, again illustrative, it reaches about $660,000 by 65; closing the rest takes about $600 a month for twenty years. Shrink the gap and every later number falls.

### Building up, then drawing down

While you are adding money, a bad year is cheap: there are decades to recover. Once withdrawals start, the order of good and bad years matters. Selling holdings to pay for groceries in a down market locks in the loss. Two retirees with the same average return can end up far apart, depending on which years came first. This is [sequence risk](/glossary#sequence-risk). It is why the mix from [Growing your money](/domains/growing-your-money) usually shifts toward steadier holdings, plus a cash cushion for a few years of spending, as retirement nears.

### How much you can take out

A [withdrawal rate](/glossary#withdrawal-rate) is the share of savings you draw in a year. No single safe number exists. A longer horizon needs a lower rate. Flexibility, the willingness to trim withdrawals after a fall, lets a higher rate survive. A large guaranteed floor makes a bad sequence survivable.

The law sets a floor of its own: from a certain age, [tax-deferred accounts](/glossary#tax-deferred-account) must pay out a minimum each year, with a penalty for missing it (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway)). Those withdrawals are taxed as income, so planned spending and the withdrawals that fund it are different numbers; [Tax efficiency](/domains/tax-efficiency) covers the rates and which account to draw first.

### Gaps, levers, and the risk underneath

Leaving work before Medicare, the federal health program, begins opens a coverage gap, bridged by a spouse's plan, [continuing the old employer's plan for a limited time](/tools/this-years-figures#financial-defense), or buying a plan on the individual market (see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway)).

Part of savings can buy an [annuity](/glossary#annuity), an insurer's promise of a fixed monthly payment for life, raising the guaranteed floor. The trade: the money is no longer yours to spend or leave behind, and the payment may not rise with inflation. Working longer, or part-time, moves everything at once: fewer years to fund, more years to save, and a larger federal benefit.

Underneath all of it is [longevity risk](/glossary#longevity-risk), the chance of living longer than the money was planned to last. It is why the floor matters, and why a plan that only works to average life expectancy fails half the people who make it.

## Common misconceptions

- **The federal benefit will cover retirement.** It replaces a fraction of pay for most people: the floor, not the plan.
- **Claiming early gets you more.** It gets you more checks, each smaller for life.
- **Retirement is one number.** It is a yearly gap, for an unknown number of years, and either side can move.
