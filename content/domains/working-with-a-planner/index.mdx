# Working with a planner

Working with a planner is the knowledge area about hiring help: what a professional adds, what it costs, what they owe you, and how to tell a good arrangement from a bad one. This page is about when a second person is worth paying for.

## Key ideas

### What a planner does and does not do

A planner runs the seven-step process on this site with you. The hardest part to get anywhere else is the outside view: a second person who sees your blind spots, questions your [assumptions](/glossary), and slows down a decision you are about to make on emotion, such as the sale after a bad month or the purchase after a raise. [Money and behavior](/domains/money-and-behavior) explains why no one can supply that check for themselves.

A planner is not a stock picker, and one who leads with picks is selling something else. Not a guarantee: no one can promise a return. And not a substitute for your own decisions. You sign, you pay, and you live with the result.

### How planners are paid, and why it matters

Pay shapes advice, whoever the person. There are three basic models and many blends.

| Model | Who pays | What it rewards | The conflict it creates |
|---|---|---|---|
| A [commission](/glossary) on products sold | The product's provider, out of the money you put in | Selling, and selling the products that pay most | Advice that favors a product over a cheaper one, or over no product at all |
| A percentage of [assets](/glossary) managed | You, deducted from your account each quarter | Gathering and keeping your investable money | Advice against paying off debt, buying a home, or moving money the planner cannot see |
| A flat or hourly fee | You, by invoice | Time and defined work | Fewer; the risk is work scoped too narrowly or done in a hurry |

Blends are common, such as a flat fee for the plan plus commissions on insurance.

Take Marcus, who has $300,000 to invest and gets the same advice from two planners: spread it across a low-cost mix and leave it alone. Under a one percent asset fee, that advice costs $3,000 the first year and about $30,000 over ten, before growth. Under a flat fee of $2,000 for the plan and $500 for each yearly check-in, it costs about $7,000 over the same ten years. The percentage model includes ongoing management, which may be worth it. Marcus can only judge that if he sees both numbers.

### The two standards of care

Two duties exist in law, and the difference is time. Some professionals owe you an ongoing duty to put your interests ahead of their own across the whole relationship. This is a [fiduciary duty](/glossary). It belongs to people and firms registered as investment advisers, under the federal law barring adviser fraud and the court decisions reading loyalty into it. Others owe a duty at the moment they make a recommendation: to act in your best interest and to manage their [conflicts of interest](/glossary), with no continuing duty afterward. This applies to brokerage firms and the people licensed to sell securities for them, under the SEC's best-interest rule.

One person can wear both hats, registered as an adviser for the plan and licensed as a broker for the products. Which duty applies depends on the hat, so ask for each account.

### What to ask before hiring

- How are you paid, in total, including anything a third party pays you?
- What conflicts of interest do you have, and where are they written down?
- Which standard of care do you owe me, and for which accounts?
- Who are your clients, usually, and how am I like or unlike them?
- What happens if I leave, and what does it cost?

A good planner answers all of these in writing.

### How to check a person's record

Two public regulator lookup tools cover most professionals. The SEC's adviser search shows whether a person or firm is registered as an investment adviser, their employment history, and any reported disciplinary events. FINRA's broker search does the same for brokers and their firms, including customer complaints. Check both. Registration makes the record public; it does not make it clean.

Firms serving retail clients must also give you a [relationship summary](/glossary), a short document stating their services, fees, conflicts, standard of care, and any disciplinary history. Read it before the first meeting.

### What a good engagement looks like

A written scope: which goals, which accounts, which steps. What you bring: the documents from [Take stock](/process/take-stock) and honest answers. What you get: a written plan, a named person, and each recommendation with its reason. How often you meet: once or twice a year, plus after any life change. How it ends: in writing, with your records returned and any ongoing fee stopped on a stated date. Money held for you sits with an independent [custodian](/glossary) in your own name, never the planner's.

### Doing it yourself, and when to walk away

Doing it yourself is reasonable when the situation is simple, you have the time and interest, and you will [check your own reasoning against this site](/process) and a second pair of eyes. It stops being reasonable with complexity, such as a business or an inheritance, a large one-time decision that cannot be undone, or a life change that resets the plan. Then a single paid hour buys a check.

Walk away from anyone who pressures you to decide today, is vague about pay, wants your money held in their own name, promises a return, or will not put scope, fees, or advice in writing.

## Where it shows up in the process

- [Take stock](/process/take-stock): Asks for source documents, reconciles them with your memory, and records what is missing.
- [Set goals](/process/set-goals): Turns wishes into amounts and dates, and surfaces goals you had not said aloud.
- [Weigh the options](/process/weigh-the-options): Sizes each gap, adds routes you had not considered, and prices them.
- [Build the plan](/process/build-the-plan): Drafts the plan and states the assumptions behind it.
- [Review together](/process/review-together): Walks you through the draft and asks for a [sign-off](/glossary) only once you can explain it yourself.
- [Put it in motion](/process/put-it-in-motion): Prepares the paperwork and chases the slow tasks. Only you can sign.
- [Stay on course](/process/stay-on-course): Runs the scheduled [review](/glossary) and talks you out of reacting.

## Common misconceptions

- **A planner's job is to beat the market.** The job is a plan that survives the market.
- **Paying nothing means it costs nothing.** A commission comes out of your money first.
- **Fee-only means conflict-free.** It removes the largest conflicts, not all of them.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
Here the subject is your own engagement. Deliver the relationship summary and fee schedule before the first substantive meeting. Put the scope, the standard of care on each account, and every conflict in the engagement letter in plain words. Document each recommendation with its reason, the alternatives considered, and its cost under your pay model. When you act as the check on a client's emotion, record what they wanted to do and what they decided.
</Callout>
