# Weigh the options

Weighing the options is the third step of the planning process. You set the facts from [Take stock](/process/take-stock) beside the ranked list from [Set goals](/process/set-goals) and look at the gap between them. Then, for each gap, you lay out the ways it could be closed and compare them. Nothing is decided yet. The result is a short list of realistic routes for each goal, ready for the plan to choose from.

## Why it matters

The first idea that comes to mind is rarely the only one. Most people close a gap the way they have seen it closed before, usually by saving harder, without noticing that the same goal could be reached by spending less, earning more, waiting longer, or wanting less. Seeing the routes side by side is the only way to know what you give up by choosing one.

It also makes the plan yours. A route you picked after comparing it with the alternatives is one you will keep following when it gets dull. A route you were handed is one you will drop at the first hard month.

## What actually happens

### Sizing each gap

For each goal on your ranked list, put the amount and date next to what you have already set aside for it and what you are adding. The difference is the gap, which planners call the [shortfall](/glossary#shortfall). Write each one down, roughly, before you try to fix any of them.

### Listing the ways to close it

There are more routes to a goal than most people think. For each gap, run through the list:

- Save more toward it.
- Spend less elsewhere, and redirect the difference.
- Earn more, through a raise, a second income, or a change of job.
- Delay the goal, so the same saving has longer to work.
- Shrink the goal, so it needs less.
- Borrow, and pay for the goal over time instead of ahead of it.
- Protect against the risk instead of funding it, where the goal is really a fear of a loss.
- Move money you already have to somewhere it serves the goal better.

Most gaps close through two or three of these together. Write down every route that could apply, even the ones you dislike.

### Counting the cost of doing nothing

Doing nothing is also a route, and it belongs on the list. It is the one you are on right now. Its cost is what the gap becomes if nothing changes: a goal reached late, reached smaller, or not at all. Planners call the value of the road not taken the [opportunity cost](/glossary#opportunity-cost). Putting it on the page turns "later" into a choice you can compare with the others.

### Comparing routes on four things

Compare each route roughly on cost, what it takes from you in money, time, or effort; time, how long until the goal is reached; risk, how much the outcome depends on things you do not control; and how easy it is to reverse if you change your mind next year. Most routes score well on three and badly on one. Note the weak point; the plan will have to manage it.

### Ruling out what will not fit you

Now apply your own constraints. Your health, your family, how steady your job is, and your temperament all rule some routes out, and that is a fair reason. A second income is not open to someone already caring for a parent. Borrowing is a poor fit for an income that swings from month to month. Cross those off. What is left is a short list of two or three realistic routes for each gap.

## Questions to ask yourself

- For my top goal, what is the gap, and what am I doing about it?
- Which route have I been assuming is the only one?
- What happens to this goal if I change nothing for five years?
- Which route could I undo next year, and which could I not?
- If none of the routes is perfect, which is good enough to start with?

## Common mistakes

- **Grabbing the first option that works.** The first route that closes the gap on paper is rarely the cheapest, safest, or easiest to undo. List the others before you commit.
- **Refusing to choose because none is perfect.** Every route costs something. Waiting for one that costs nothing is the do-nothing route with a better excuse.
- **Comparing on money alone.** A route that saves the most but that you will not stick to closes no gap at all.
- **Hunting for the one right answer.** This step ends with a short list of good routes, not a winner. Choosing is the next step's job.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
At this step you are widening the client's view, not narrowing it. For each prioritized goal, present at least two routes alongside the do-nothing case. Document the alternatives considered, including those rejected and the constraint that rejected them, so the file shows the reasoning and not only the recommendation. Note which routes the client resisted; that resistance will surface again during implementation.
</Callout>
