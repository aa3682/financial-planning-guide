# Take stock

Taking stock is the first step of the planning process. You gather the facts about where you stand today, before you decide anything about where you want to go: what you earn, what you spend, what you own, what you owe, what protection you already have, and where the paperwork lives. Nothing gets judged yet. The only job is to see clearly.

## Why it matters

Every later step rests on this one. A goal set on a guessed income is a guess. A plan that leaves out a forgotten debt breaks the first time that debt comes due.

Most people carry a mental picture of their finances that is a little out of date, a little too rosy, or missing a piece. That is normal. Bank apps, employer portals, and insurance policies each hold one slice, and nobody sends you the whole picture. Taking stock assembles the slices in one place, so the plan works from what is real, not what is remembered.

As you go, keep a short list of surprises: anything where a document said something different from what you expected. That list is not a scorecard. Each surprise marks a place where a plan built from memory would have gone wrong.

It also sets a baseline. When you [stay on course](/process/stay-on-course) later, you can only see what changed if you recorded the start.

## What actually happens

### Income and spending

For income, pull pay stubs, tax returns, and statements for any side work, rental, or benefit payments. For spending, use the last several months of bank and card statements rather than a budget written from memory. Statements show the real pattern, including the subscriptions and small habits a budget leaves out.

### Assets and debts

List everything you own that has value, which planners call your assets: cash, bank accounts, retirement and investment accounts, property, vehicles. Then list everything you owe, your liabilities: mortgage, car loans, student loans, credit cards, money borrowed from family. Next to each debt, note the interest rate, the monthly payment, and when it ends. What you own minus what you owe is your [net worth](/glossary#net-worth), the one number this step produces.

### Insurance in force

Gather every policy you currently hold: health, life, disability, home or renters, auto, and any coverage through your employer. "In force" simply means the policy is active and paid up. For each one, note what it covers, how much, what you pay, and when it renews. Many people find a policy they forgot, or a gap they assumed was covered.

### Accounts and who owns them

For each account, write down whose name is on it, whether it is held jointly, and who is listed as the [beneficiary](/glossary#beneficiary), the person who receives it if you die. Ownership decides who can reach the money, how it is taxed, and where it goes later. An old retirement account with a former partner still named as beneficiary is a common and fixable find.

### Key documents

Locate wills, powers of attorney, trust documents, property deeds, loan agreements, and recent tax returns. You do not need to read them closely yet. You need to know they exist, are current, and where they are. A document nobody can find in a crisis is no document.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Where does my income come from, and how steady is each source?
- What did I spend last month, according to the statements?
- What do I own, what do I owe, and what is the difference?
- What would happen to my income if I could not work for six months?
- Whose name is on each account, and who is named to receive it?
- Where are my important documents, and does anyone else know?
- Which of these answers surprised me?

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing precision before accuracy.** Valuing your car to the dollar while forgetting a credit card entirely. A complete picture that is roughly right beats a partial one that is exact. Get every item on the page first; refine later.
- **Listing the budget instead of the spending.** A budget is what you intend. Statements are what happened.
- **Skipping the uncomfortable items.** The card you avoid opening, the loan from a relative, the account you have not logged into since the last job. These are exactly the items a plan needs. Leaving them out only delays them.
- **Treating the exercise as a verdict.** The numbers describe a situation, not a character. Many people find this step embarrassing, then find it was the most useful thing they did all year. Whatever the documents show, you now know it.
- **Doing it once and never again.** A snapshot ages. Keep the list; the last step of the process brings you back here.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
At this step you are collecting, not advising, and the client should feel that. Ask for source documents rather than the client's summary of them, and reconcile the two without comment on the gap. Record the date of every figure, which items are documented and which are estimates, and each account's registration and beneficiary designation as shown on the custodian's records. Note anything the client could not produce, with a follow-up date. This file is the baseline every later review is measured against, so complete it before setting a single goal.
</Callout>
