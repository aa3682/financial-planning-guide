# Set goals

Setting goals is the second step of the planning process. You take the facts from [Take stock](/process/take-stock) and decide what the money is for: what you want, how much each goal needs, by when, and which comes first when they compete. The result is a short, ranked list a plan can work toward.

## Why it matters

A plan is a set of instructions for money, and vague goals produce vague instructions. "Save more" cannot tell you how much to put aside this month. Every later step needs goals that can.

Goals set before you take stock are wishes. They float free of income, debt, and what is already saved. Once the facts are on the page, some wishes turn out closer than you thought and some further. That is not a failure; it is the first useful thing the plan tells you.

Goals also change. A job moves, a child arrives, a parent needs care, and last year's list no longer fits. That is why the ranking matters more than the exact list. If you know what comes first and why, you can rewrite the list without starting over.

## What actually happens

### From wishes to goals

Start wide. Write down everything you would like money to do for you, including the ones you are embarrassed by or think are out of reach. A goal you never write down still shapes your choices, just in the dark.

Then give each one three parts. An amount: how much it needs, even as a rough figure. A date: when you need the money, which sets the goal's [time horizon](/glossary#time-horizon), the number of years between now and then. And a reason: what it is for, in one sentence. The reason is what you check against when goals compete, and what tells you whether a goal still fits after your life changes.

### Needs, wants, and nice-to-haves

Needs are goals whose failure would do real harm: a cash reserve for emergencies, a debt growing faster than you can pay it, income for the years you cannot work. Wants would make life noticeably better but leave you safe if delayed. Nice-to-haves are the rest: real, worth keeping, and the first to give ground. A want dressed up as a need crowds out the actual needs.

### Ranking goals that compete

Most goals draw on the same money, so ranking is unavoidable. Within each group, put the goals in order. When two compete, ask which costs more to delay. A fixed, near date with a hard consequence usually goes first. The finished list is a single order, first funded to last.

### Goals you share

If you share money with someone, you share goals. Each person writes their own list first, then you compare. The aim is to find the goals you both hold, agree on the ranking for the shared money, and be clear about which goals belong to one person alone. An unspoken disagreement about goals surfaces later as a fight about spending.

### Room for what you have not thought of yet

The list will be incomplete. Some goals only become visible after a change in your life. Leave room: a share of what you save with no name on it yet, and a habit of revisiting the list when you [stay on course](/process/stay-on-course). A plan with no slack breaks at the first unplanned goal.

## Questions to ask yourself

- What would I want money to do for me if nobody else would see the list?
- Which goal have I been avoiding writing down, and why?
- If two goals need the same money next year, which one wins?
- What would I give up, starting this month, to fund my top goal?
- Which of my goals does my partner or family not know about?

## Common mistakes

- **Setting goals before the facts.** A goal chosen without knowing income, debt, and savings is a wish. Check it against the numbers and be ready to change it.
- **Skipping the amount or the date.** "Someday" and "enough" are not goals a plan can use.
- **Ranking by excitement.** The most appealing goal is rarely the one that costs most to delay. Fund the boring need before the exciting want.
- **Keeping the real goal private.** The goal you did not tell your partner about is the one behind the spending they cannot explain.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
At this step you are drawing goals out, not supplying them. Listen for the goal behind the stated one, and write goals in the client's words before translating them into figures. For couples, take each list separately and record where they differ. Document each goal with its amount, date, reason, priority group, and the client's own ranking. Note any goal that conflicts with the inventory.
</Callout>
