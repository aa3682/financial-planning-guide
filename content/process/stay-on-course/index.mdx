# Stay on course

Staying on course is the seventh and last step of the planning process. At intervals you check two things: whether the plan you put in motion actually happened, and whether the world it was built for still exists. Then you adjust, or you go back to an earlier step.

## Why it matters

A plan describes a moment. Every month after it is written, something drifts: a transfer is skipped, a raise arrives, a goal stops mattering. Without a check, the plan on paper and the life it serves part ways quietly, and nobody notices until a goal comes due.

You wrote down assumptions in [Build the plan](/process/build-the-plan) and kept a done list in [Put it in motion](/process/put-it-in-motion) so that both could be tested. A review on a schedule, against those written facts, gets the plan changed for reasons. A review whenever the news is loud gets it changed for feelings.

## What actually happens

### Two checks, not one

The first check asks whether the plan happened. Take the done list and go through the plan block by block. A block still waiting after months is a task to finish, not a plan to rewrite.

The second asks whether the plan still fits. Take the [assumptions](/glossary), the guesses the plan rests on, and beside each write what actually happened: income, costs, growth, dates. These two lists are the whole [review](/glossary), the scheduled comparison of the plan with what happened.

### Two triggers

A review happens for one of two reasons. The first is a date: a fixed date you choose, at least once a year, kept whether or not anything feels different. The second is a life change: a job change, a marriage or separation, a birth or a death, an inheritance, a move, a change in health.

The date is easy to keep. The life change is the one people miss, because it arrives with its own demands and the plan is the last thing anyone thinks of. Yet it matters more, since it usually rewrites an assumption outright.

### Adjusting versus going back

Most reviews end in small adjustments: an amount changes because income did, a date moves because a cost did, a route changes because the one chosen stopped fitting.

A review sends you back when something bigger has moved. A goal is gone or a new one has appeared: that is [Set goals](/process/set-goals). Two goals no longer both fit: that is [Weigh the options](/process/weigh-the-options). The picture you started from was wrong: that is [Take stock](/process/take-stock). The test is whether the change fits inside a block, or changes what the blocks are.

### Adjusting is not reacting

An adjustment answers a change in your facts. A reaction answers a change in your mood. A bad month in the markets is the usual case. A plan for a goal with a long [time horizon](/glossary), the years between now and when the money is needed, expected bad months. They were part of the growth assumption, not a failure of it. Unless the check shows an assumption actually broken, a rough stretch is a reason to hold, not to move.

### Starting again

A review begins the way the process began: by taking stock. The picture you recorded at the first step is your [baseline](/glossary), the starting point later measurements are compared against. Update it, compare the two, and you are back at step one with better information.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Did each block of the plan get done, and if not, why?
- Which assumptions turned out wrong, and by how much?
- What has changed in my life since the last review?
- Am I changing the plan because my facts changed, or because I am worried?

## Common mistakes

- **Reviewing only when something feels wrong.** Worry skips the quiet years when drift builds and shows up in the loud ones.
- **Skipping the done list.** Checking assumptions on a plan that was never carried out tests nothing.
- **Treating a bad quarter as a broken assumption.** The assumption was about years. A quarter is not evidence against it.
- **Fixing a goal problem with an amount change.** If a goal no longer makes sense, raising the contribution only funds it faster.

## For advisors

<Callout type="info">
At this step you are monitoring, and the client should know what triggers a review and how to tell you when one happens. Document the review date and its trigger, each assumption with the recorded figure beside the actual, each adjustment with its reason and date, and any item sent back to an earlier step and why. Note how the client reacted to market moves between meetings; it says more about their tolerance for risk than a calm conversation.
</Callout>
