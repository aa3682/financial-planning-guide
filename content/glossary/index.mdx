# Glossary

This page defines every term the guide links to, in plain English and for education rather than advice. Any number a term depends on, such as a limit, rate, or age, lives on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures) rather than here.

### Anchoring [#anchoring]

The habit of letting the first number you see, such as a list price or a past account balance, pull your judgment toward it even when it has no bearing on the decision. Investors anchor on what they paid for something when deciding whether to sell it.

### Annuity [#annuity]

A contract with an insurance company in which you pay a lump sum or a series of payments and the insurer promises payments back to you, often for the rest of your life. It is one way to turn savings into [guaranteed income](#guaranteed-income).

### APR [#apr]

Annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of borrowing expressed as a single percentage that includes interest and most required fees. It lets you compare loans with different fee structures on the same footing.

### Asset allocation [#asset-allocation]

How your investments are divided among broad categories such as [stocks](#stock), [bonds](#bond), and cash. The split drives more of the risk and return you experience than any single pick within a category.

### Asset location [#asset-location]

The decision about which type of account should hold which investment, so that the most heavily taxed holdings sit in the accounts that shelter them best. It is separate from [asset allocation](#asset-allocation), which decides how much of each investment you own overall.

### Assets [#assets]

Everything you own that has monetary value: cash, accounts, investments, property, and the cash value of some insurance. Your assets minus your [liabilities](#liabilities) is your net worth.

### Assumptions [#assumptions]

The estimates a plan rests on that no one can know in advance, such as future returns, inflation, pay growth, and how long you will live. A plan is only as reliable as its assumptions, so write them down and revisit them.

### Automatic transfer [#automatic-transfer]

A standing instruction to move a set amount between accounts on a schedule, such as from checking to savings on payday. It puts a savings decision on autopilot so it happens without willpower.

### Baseline [#baseline]

A snapshot of where your finances stand today, before any changes: what you own, what you owe, what comes in, and what goes out. Later progress is measured against it.

### Beneficiary [#beneficiary]

The person or organization you name to receive an account, insurance payout, or other asset when you die. The name on the form usually overrides whatever your will says about the same asset.

### Bond [#bond]

A loan you make to a government or company in exchange for regular interest payments and the return of the amount lent on a set date. Bonds generally swing less than stocks, but they lose value when interest rates rise or the borrower runs into trouble.

### Capital gain [#capital-gain]

The profit when you sell an investment for more than its [cost basis](#cost-basis). How long you held it decides which tax rate applies; the holding period and the rate tiers are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Cash flow [#cash-flow]

The money that comes in and goes out over a period, usually a month. Positive cash flow means you spend less than you earn, and it is the raw material every other goal is built from.

### Cash reserve [#cash-reserve]

Money kept in a safe, quickly accessible account to cover an emergency or a gap in income without borrowing or selling investments. How many months of expenses to hold is a judgment call, not a rule.

### Commission [#commission]

A payment a salesperson or firm receives for selling you a product, usually a share of the amount invested or the premium paid. It is one way advice gets paid for, and it creates a [conflict of interest](#conflict-of-interest) worth knowing about.

### Compounding [#compounding]

Earning returns on your earlier returns, not just on the money you put in. Over long periods it is why starting early matters more than the size of any single contribution.

### Conflict of interest [#conflict-of-interest]

A situation in which the person advising you would benefit from steering you one way rather than another. It does not mean the advice is wrong, but you should know the conflict exists before you weigh the advice.

### Cost basis [#cost-basis]

What you paid for an investment, including any commissions, adjusted for events such as reinvested dividends. Gain or loss at sale is measured from it.

### Credit [#credit]

On a tax return, an amount subtracted directly from the tax you owe, which makes it worth more than a [deduction](#deduction) of the same size. In borrowing, the word means your ability to obtain a loan.

### Credit report [#credit-report]

A record kept by a consumer reporting company of your borrowing history: accounts, balances, payment record, and public records such as bankruptcies. You are entitled to free copies on a schedule set by law; see [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#money-basics).

### Credit score [#credit-score]

A number computed from your credit report that lenders use to predict how likely you are to repay. Different scoring models produce different numbers from the same report.

### Custodian [#custodian]

The firm that holds your investments and cash, keeps the records, and sends the statements. When a planner recommends trades, the custodian is where the money actually sits, and the two are usually separate businesses.

### Deductible [#deductible]

The amount you pay out of pocket on a claim before your insurance starts paying. A higher deductible lowers the premium because you are keeping more of the risk yourself.

### Deduction [#deduction]

An amount subtracted from your income before tax is figured, so its value depends on your [marginal rate](#marginal-rate). It reduces the income that is taxed, not the tax itself.

### Default [#default]

In behavior, the option that applies when you do nothing, such as the contribution rate a workplace plan enrolls you at. Defaults are powerful because most people never change them. In borrowing, the word means failing to repay as agreed.

### Deposit insurance [#deposit-insurance]

A federal guarantee that money in an insured bank or credit union account is repaid if the institution fails, up to a limit per depositor and ownership category. The limit is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#money-basics).

### Diversification [#diversification]

Spreading money across many investments so that no single company, industry, or country can sink the whole portfolio. It reduces the risk that comes from any one holding; it does not remove the risk that the whole market falls.

### Effective rate [#effective-rate]

The share of your total income that actually went to tax, found by dividing the tax you paid by your income. It is always lower than your [marginal rate](#marginal-rate), because the lower tiers of income are taxed at lower rates.

### Elimination period [#elimination-period]

The waiting time after a disability begins before a disability policy starts paying benefits. It works like a deductible measured in time: a longer wait means a lower premium, but you need savings to bridge it.

### Employer match [#employer-match]

Money an employer adds to your workplace retirement account based on what you contribute, up to a stated share of your pay. Contributing less than the full match leaves part of your pay on the table.

### Estate [#estate]

Everything you own at death, before debts are paid and property is passed on. Whether federal estate tax applies depends on the size of the estate against an exclusion amount; see [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#passing-on-wealth).

### Estimated payments [#estimated-payments]

Tax you send directly to the tax agency in installments during the year when withholding will not cover what you owe, as with self-employment or investment income. The due dates and the safe levels are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Exclusions [#exclusions]

The events, causes, or property a policy states it will not cover. Reading them tells you which risks you still carry yourself after buying the policy.

### Executor [#executor]

The person named in a will to carry out its instructions: gathering assets, paying debts and taxes, and distributing what remains. Some states call this person the personal representative.

### Expected return [#expected-return]

The average yearly gain an investment is projected to deliver over a long period, given its risk. It is a planning input, not a promise; the return in any single year will land well above or below it.

### Expense ratio [#expense-ratio]

The yearly cost of owning a fund, stated as a share of the amount you have invested and taken out of the fund's returns automatically. Lower is better, and the difference compounds over decades.

### Fiduciary duty [#fiduciary-duty]

A legal obligation to act in your best interest and put it ahead of the adviser's own, with loyalty and care. Registered investment advisers owe it across the whole relationship; other financial professionals owe you different, narrower duties.

### Funding order [#funding-order]

The sequence in which you direct spare dollars among competing goals, such as debt, a cash reserve, a matched retirement plan, and other savings. A written order turns a hard monthly judgment into a routine.

### Guaranteed income [#guaranteed-income]

Money that arrives on a schedule regardless of how markets perform, such as Social Security, a pension, or payments from an annuity. It covers the spending you cannot afford to have interrupted. The ages at which the federal retirement benefit can start are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway).

### Guardian [#guardian]

The person your will names to raise your minor children if no parent is living. A court makes the final appointment, but it gives heavy weight to the name in the will.

### Health-care directive [#health-care-directive]

A document that states the medical treatment you do and do not want and names someone to make medical decisions if you cannot. States use different forms and names for it, but the purpose is the same.

### Herding [#herding]

Following what other people are doing with their money because they are doing it, rather than because it fits your plan. It drives buying at peaks and selling at bottoms.

### Implementation [#implementation]

The step in which a plan's recommendations actually get carried out: accounts opened, transfers set up, policies bought, documents signed. A plan that is not implemented has changed nothing.

### Index fund [#index-fund]

A [pooled fund](#pooled-fund) that holds the same investments as a published market index, in the same proportions, rather than paying a manager to pick. The result is broad diversification at low cost.

### Inflation [#inflation]

The general rise in prices over time, which means a dollar buys less next year than it does today. It is measured by a government price index; [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#money-basics) points to the current reading.

### Interest rate [#interest-rate]

The price of borrowing money, stated as a share of the amount borrowed per year. When you save, it is the price someone pays you for the use of your money.

### Investing [#investing]

Putting money into assets whose value can rise or fall, such as stocks, bonds, and property, in exchange for a chance at growth over time. It differs from [saving](#saving) in that you accept the possibility of loss.

### Liabilities [#liabilities]

Everything you owe: mortgages, car loans, student loans, credit card balances, and unpaid taxes. They are subtracted from [assets](#assets) to find net worth.

### Liability [#liability]

Your legal responsibility to pay for harm you cause to another person or their property. Liability insurance covers those payments; the minimum coverage your state requires for a car is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#financial-defense).

### Longevity risk [#longevity-risk]

The chance of outliving your money because you live longer than your plan assumed. It is the reason planning uses a long horizon and leans on guaranteed income for essential spending.

### Loss aversion [#loss-aversion]

The tendency to feel a loss more strongly than a gain of the same size. It leads people to hold losing investments too long and to shy away from reasonable risk.

### Marginal rate [#marginal-rate]

The tax rate that applies to your next dollar of income, set by the tier that dollar falls into. It is the rate to use when judging a deduction or an extra dollar of earnings; the tiers themselves are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Mental accounting [#mental-accounting]

Treating money differently depending on where it came from or what you have labeled it for, such as spending a bonus freely while budgeting a paycheck tightly. Dollars are interchangeable; the labels are in your head. Labels help when they serve a plan and hurt when they hide the full picture.

### Net worth [#net-worth]

What you own minus what you owe, at one point in time. It is the single number that tells you whether your finances are moving in the right direction from year to year.

### Opportunity cost [#opportunity-cost]

What you give up by choosing one use of money over another, such as the growth you forgo by paying off a low-rate loan instead of investing. Every dollar has one whether you count it or not.

### Overconfidence [#overconfidence]

Believing your judgment or knowledge is better than it is, which leads to trading too often and holding too concentrated a portfolio. It is common among experienced people, not just beginners.

### Pension [#pension]

A retirement plan in which an employer promises a set monthly payment for life, based on your pay and years of service, and carries the investment risk itself. It is increasingly rare outside government work.

### Policy limit [#policy-limit]

The most an insurance policy will pay for a claim, or for all claims in a period. Losses above the limit are yours to pay.

### Pooled fund [#pooled-fund]

An investment that gathers money from many people and buys a portfolio of holdings on their behalf, so each investor owns a slice of the whole. Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds are the common forms.

### Power of attorney [#power-of-attorney]

A document that gives someone you choose legal authority to handle your financial affairs, either right away or only if you become unable to. Without one, your family may need a court order to manage your accounts.

### Premium [#premium]

The price you pay, usually monthly or yearly, to keep an insurance policy in force. It reflects the insurer's estimate of your risk plus its costs.

### Present bias [#present-bias]

Giving far more weight to what you get now than to what you get later, even when waiting is clearly better. It is why saving feels hard and why automatic transfers work.

### Probate [#probate]

The court process that confirms a will is valid and supervises paying debts and distributing the estate. It is public and can take months; assets with a named beneficiary or a joint owner skip it.

### Realized [#realized]

Describes a gain or loss that has been locked in by selling the investment. Until you sell, a gain exists only on paper and generally is not taxed.

### Rebalancing [#rebalancing]

Selling some of what has grown and buying more of what has lagged, to return your portfolio to its target asset allocation. It keeps your risk where you set it and forces a sell-high, buy-low discipline.

### Recency bias [#recency-bias]

Assuming that what has happened lately will keep happening, such as expecting a rising market to keep rising. It makes the most recent years feel like the whole picture.

### Relationship summary [#relationship-summary]

A short disclosure that brokerage firms and investment advisers must give you describing their services, fees, conflicts, and disciplinary history. Reading it is the fastest way to learn how a professional is paid.

### Retitling [#retitling]

Changing the legal owner named on an account, deed, or other asset, for example moving a house into a trust or adding a spouse to an account. It is how ownership itself moves, separate from what a will says.

### Review [#review]

A scheduled check of a plan against what has actually happened, to catch drift and update assumptions. Life changes, not market moves, are the usual reason to adjust.

### Right of survivorship [#right-of-survivorship]

A feature of joint ownership under which the surviving owner automatically takes the whole asset when the other owner dies. The asset passes outside the will and outside probate.

### Risk [#risk]

The chance that an outcome differs from what you expected, including the chance of losing money. In investing, higher expected return comes with higher risk; the aim is to take only the risk your goals need and you can tolerate.

### Risk pooling [#risk-pooling]

The principle behind insurance: many people each pay a small, known amount so that the few who suffer a large loss are paid from the pool. It works because the timing of a loss is unpredictable for any one person but predictable across a group.

### Risk tolerance [#risk-tolerance]

How much drop in value you can live with, financially and emotionally, without abandoning your plan. It is part capacity, meaning what your finances can absorb, and part temperament.

### Rollover [#rollover]

Moving money from one retirement account to another, such as from a former employer's plan to an individual account, without it counting as a taxable withdrawal. A direct transfer between institutions avoids the withholding and the deadline that apply when the money passes through your hands.

### Saving [#saving]

Setting money aside in a place where the amount will not fall, such as a bank account, so it is there when you need it. Saving protects value; [investing](#investing) tries to grow it.

### Savings rate [#savings-rate]

The share of your income that you set aside rather than spend, usually figured against gross or take-home pay. Over a working life it matters more than investment skill.

### Sequence risk [#sequence-risk]

The danger that poor returns arrive early in retirement, when you are drawing money out, so the portfolio shrinks before it can recover. Two retirees with the same average return can end up in very different places depending on the order.

### Shortfall [#shortfall]

The gap between what a goal will cost and what your current path will provide. Naming the shortfall in dollars turns a vague worry into a problem you can act on.

### Sign-off [#sign-off]

The point at which you and anyone else the plan affects agree to the recommendations before anything is put in motion. It is the moment to raise doubts, because [implementation](#implementation) follows.

### Stakeholders [#stakeholders]

Everyone whose life the plan touches and whose agreement it needs: a spouse or partner, dependents, sometimes aging parents or a business partner. A plan built for one person in a household of two rarely holds.

### Standard deduction [#standard-deduction]

A fixed amount every filer can subtract from income instead of listing individual deductions. You take it or itemize, whichever is larger; the amount is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Stock [#stock]

A share of ownership in a company, which gives you a claim on its future profits. Stocks have delivered the highest long-run returns of the major asset classes and the largest swings along the way.

### Tax-advantaged account [#tax-advantaged-account]

Any account the tax law rewards for a purpose such as retirement, education, or health costs, by deferring tax on contributions, exempting growth, or both. Each comes with yearly contribution limits and withdrawal rules; the retirement account limits are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway).

### Tax-deferred account [#tax-deferred-account]

An account where contributions may reduce your taxable income now and growth is not taxed until you withdraw, at which point withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. The law requires withdrawals to begin at a set age; see [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway).

### Tax-loss harvesting [#tax-loss-harvesting]

Selling an investment at a loss to offset gains elsewhere, then reinvesting so your allocation does not change. Buying back the same investment too soon voids the loss; the window and the yearly offset limit are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#tax-efficiency).

### Taxable account [#taxable-account]

An ordinary investment account with no special tax treatment: you pay tax on interest, dividends, and [realized](#realized) gains in the year they occur. Its advantage is that there are no limits on contributions or withdrawals.

### Time horizon [#time-horizon]

How long until you need the money for a goal. The longer the horizon, the more risk you can reasonably take, because there is time to recover from a bad stretch.

### Transfer on death [#transfer-on-death]

A designation on an account, or in some states on a vehicle or real estate title, that names who receives it when you die. Like a beneficiary form, it passes the asset outside the will and outside probate.

### Trust [#trust]

A legal arrangement in which one person holds property for the benefit of another, under written instructions. Trusts can avoid probate, manage money for someone who cannot, or control how and when heirs receive it.

### Trustee [#trustee]

The person or institution responsible for managing a trust's property according to its terms and in the beneficiaries' interest. A trustee owes a [fiduciary duty](#fiduciary-duty) and can be held to account for breaking it.

### Vesting [#vesting]

Earning a permanent right to money an employer has put into your retirement account, usually after a set period of service. Your own contributions are always yours; the law caps how long an employer can make you wait for theirs, and the cap is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#retirement-runway).

### Will [#will]

A legal document that says who receives your property at death, who will settle your affairs, and who should raise your minor children. It controls only assets that do not pass by beneficiary form, joint ownership, or trust.

### Withdrawal rate [#withdrawal-rate]

The share of your portfolio you take out each year in retirement, usually stated against the starting balance. A sustainable rate depends on horizon, allocation, and flexibility, and no single number fits everyone.

### Withholding [#withholding]

Tax your employer or other payer takes out of each payment and sends to the tax agency on your behalf, as a prepayment of the year's bill. Adjusting it is how you avoid a large refund or a surprise balance due.
